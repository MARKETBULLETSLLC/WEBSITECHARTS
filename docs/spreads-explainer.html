01 — Foundation
What Is a Spread?

The gap between two prices
is the market's opinion.

"Strip away the noise of daily price swings. What's left is the relationship."

A spread chart plots the difference between two prices rather than either price alone. That difference — between contract months, between exchanges, between crops — is not random. It reflects carrying costs, quality differentials, and competitive substitution pressure, updated in real time by every buyer and seller in the market.

Spreads are the market talking to itself. MarketBullets® tracks three kinds — each one answering a different question a producer needs answered before picking up the phone.

Why It Matters

A producer who reads spread behavior doesn't just know the price — they know the structure underneath it. Structure often shifts before flat price moves. That's the edge.

02 — Calendar Spread
Same Exchange · Different Contract Months

The market's live answer
to the storage question.

"Should I sell now or hold for later? The spread already decided — you're just reading the verdict."

July ZW versus December ZW: the difference between these two contracts is the carrying charge — what the market will pay you to store wheat from now through harvest and into fall. A wide positive carry says: "We have time — hold it." When that carry collapses — when the nearby price catches deferred — somebody changed their mind. They need grain now.

That urgency is one of the cleanest timing signals in soft white wheat. You won't find it on a flat-price chart.

Decision Signal

Carry collapse = tightening nearby demand. Watch for the spread narrowing as a trigger to sell nearby before the structure shifts further against storage.

03 — Inter-Market Spread
Different Exchanges · Same Timeframe

The protein elevator:
what bakers are paying today.

"Chicago versus Kansas City — the difference between crackers and bread, priced in real time."

CBOT SRW versus KCBT HRW: this spread is the live protein premium — what the market will pay for baking quality above milling quality. When it expands, milling demand is asserting itself and bakers are competing for quality wheat. When it compresses, SRW is catching up — often on export momentum from buyers who will take whatever is priced most competitively.

For PNW soft white producers, this spread maps where your class sits in the global quality hierarchy at any given moment. It's not your market — but it sets the context for whether your basis is moving for the right reasons.

Decision Signal

Collapsing HRW premium often signals SRW export demand — a potential basis-strengthening window. Expanding premium signals quality competition is driving the complex.

04 — Inter-Commodity Spread
Wheat vs. Competing Crops

Is your wheat cheap or expensive
to a feedlot buyer?

"The question most wheat producers never think to ask — until they wish they had."

The wheat-to-corn price ratio is one of the most durable arbitrage relationships in grain markets. When wheat trades below roughly 120% of corn on a comparable price basis, feed buyers start substituting wheat for corn — and that substitution demand can build a price floor underneath a declining market. A support level you won't find on a flat-price chart.

When the ratio extends the other direction — wheat at historically wide premiums — substitution risk increases and demand begins to migrate. This spread answers a question most producers never ask: "Is my wheat expensive or cheap relative to everything else a buyer can purchase today?"

Decision Signal

Wheat near or below the 1.2× threshold = potential feed demand supporting a price floor. Wide premium above that line = rationing risk. Protect the price before the ratio corrects it for you.
