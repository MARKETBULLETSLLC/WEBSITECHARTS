Start the Leg Before You Open the Trap

Gary Hofer · Waitsburg, Washington

Harvest of 1966. A private family storage siding in Clyde, Washington, fifteen dollars a day, and a twelve-year-old trusted with the elevator. Up at 5:30, breakfast, then a ride with Uncle Paul up to combine camp. The job: have the pit empty and the leg running before the first truck rolled in.

There's a rule you learn fast and never forget: always start the leg motor before you open the feeder trap. Get that backwards and you'll know it. All day you listen to the leg — the belt of buckets that hauls wheat from the boot below the floor up to the bin — and you learn its hum. A change in pitch is rarely good news.

Sweep the tanks. Close the traps. Grease the shafts. Tally the trucks. And above everything else — catch the last truck back to the house, because nobody's coming to get you until after supper.

Nobody taught commodities theory that summer. But the elevator taught what markets never put in a report: what it actually costs, in attention, to move a bushel of wheat without losing it. That's still the question underneath every chart MarketBullets publishes.

Where It Led

Along that path, one thing kept showing up: the people actually carrying the price risk — the growers, not the desks — had the least access to real intelligence about it. Institutional traders had teams, terminals, and analysts. A producer had a radio and a hunch.

REAP grew out of that — an effort to translate what the desks knew into something a producer could use before calling the elevator. MarketBullets carries the same premise forward. Its benchmark, Box‑o‑Rox, is named for a plain truth: useful signals rarely need to be complicated. Forty-four years in, the market still has lessons left to teach, and the commentary is written with that in mind.

Who It's For

A marketing decision made without good information doesn't stay small — it can show up in an operating loan for years. MarketBullets exists so the person calling the elevator at 7:30, or the merchandiser at a desk before the open, has something steadier than a hunch: verified USDA and CFTC data, read carefully and delivered before the day session opens.
