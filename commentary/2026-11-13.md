For three weeks Chicago wheat sold a peace that hadn't happened. This week the talks ended with a prisoner exchange and nothing on shipping, and the market spent four sessions working out what that meant. Dec Chicago settled Friday at $5.93½. The week's high was $6.03¾ on Wednesday and its low was $5.82 at the turn of the session Thursday evening, a range of 21¾¢. Chicago finished the week 3¢ above last Friday's $5.90½ settle. By our count that ends a ten-week losing streak. That's a draw, not a rally. The market traded 18¢ up off that low and then sat under $6.00 for the rest of the session. A draw after ten losses is worth marking on a chart. It doesn't tell us the low is in.

Here's how Friday traded. Dec Chicago was up 12¾¢ overnight, $5.95¼ at 1:58 a.m. Pacific, on the bounce that began Thursday night. It slid about 4¢ in the half hour before the export sales report posted at 5:30 a.m., and the report itself moved it only about 1¢, to a low of $5.90¼. A low number that barely registers is information too. By the 7 a.m. hour Pacific it had run to $6.00. That's the same zone that capped the market the two sessions before: Wednesday traded to $6.03¾ and settled at $6.00¾, and Thursday's high was $6.03. From $6.00 on Friday it faded for the rest of the session, and the settle was 6½¢ under the high. Kansas City Dec traded from $6.18¼ to $6.39 and settled at $6.28¾.

**The report itself.** USDA put net wheat sales for the week ended Nov. 5 at 182,844 tonnes, per its Export Sales Reporting data. That's down 66% from the 538,028 of the week before and 60% under the 462,478 of the same week last year. The trade's range was 350,000 to 650,000 tonnes, so the number came in at about half the bottom of it. Gross new sales were 198,710 tonnes, and cancellations and reductions took 15,866 tonnes off, which is how it nets to 182,844. The Philippines, last week's top buyer, was a net reducer this week, down 5,951 tonnes, and so was Ecuador, down 15,700. Mexico took 52,799 tonnes, Japan 48,441, Indonesia 44,482, Thailand 35,500 and the Dominican Republic 28,600. Shipments were better, 239,467 tonnes against 149,163 the week before. Nothing in next marketing year's column.

**Now the number that matters more.** Weekly sales are noisy. Commitments aren't. Counting shipped and unshipped wheat together, the U.S. has 12.24 million tonnes on the books against 17.27 million at this point last year, a 29% shortfall. On Tuesday we noted that USDA's forecast assumes exports fall 17%. The books are behind by almost twice that. The classes tell different stories. Hard red winter commitments are 2.53 million tonnes against 6.45 million last year, down 61%. Soft red winter is down 26%. Hard red spring is down 12%. White wheat is up 0.3%, with 3.80 million tonnes committed, and its accumulated exports are up 8.7%. This is why the Portland soft white bid has held while the Chicago board has dropped, and it's why hard red winter, cheap against the world's milling wheat, still can't find a seat at the table. White wheat had a thin week on new sales, 14,803 tonnes. It didn't have a thin year.

The dollar didn't take the week off. The dollar index closed Friday near 103.4, another high for the move, after Tuesday's hot October CPI put the odds of a December rate hike near 85%. Chicago held anyway. Reuters, in its Friday market wrap, gave the reason as continued disruption to Black Sea exports, which it said outweighed the dollar and expectations of big Australian and Argentine crops. Rosario raised its 2026/27 Argentine wheat forecast by 500,000 tonnes to 24.3 million. An analyst quoted by Reuters, Rod Baker, put the setup in two sentences: "If wheat starts flowing through the Black Sea, there's likely more downside for prices." And: "If it doesn't, we're getting close to a price floor now." Both sentences depend on the same shipping lanes, and nobody at this week's talks moved them.

**Hard red winter and Paris, as of Thursday.** Kansas City Dec settled Friday at $6.28¾, up 9¾¢ against Chicago's 11¢, and its premium over Chicago narrowed to 35¼¢ from 36½¢. The better read is against Paris, the series we track in dollars a bushel: KC minus Paris was -$1.08 at Thursday's close, which means Paris was $1.08 over Kansas City, a drop of $0.32 on the week. In mid-summer the spread stood about 80¢ the other way, with KC over Paris. The monthly average sits near -$0.75. The widest Paris premium on the weekly chart was -$1.92 in January 2025. So hard red winter went from the expensive wheat to the cheap wheat in about three months, a swing of nearly $1.90. Cheap has a way of finding buyers, and this week's report says it hasn't yet: hard red winter net sales were 62,745 tonnes, with 9,327 shipped, and commitments 61% behind. The tell to watch is the spread narrowing because KC gets bought, not because Paris gets sold. Paris and Minneapolis settle after 5 p.m. Pacific, so every Paris and Minneapolis figure here is Thursday's. Paris itself was turned back at its Box-o-Rox line near €236.75 on Wednesday and closed Thursday at €231.75.

The diplomacy moved but nothing reached a ship. Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner spent about two hours with Putin at the Kremlin on Thursday, per the Associated Press, then flew to Dublin to brief Zelenskyy, who said Europe would be part of the next round, per the Irish Times. USDA's Tuesday WASDE had already priced the damage on paper: Russia's exports cut 1 million tonnes to 39 million, Ukraine's cut to 11.5 million, and still a world carryout of 280.8 million tonnes that was above every trade guess. The wheat exists. The question is whether it moves. We have no confirmed meeting on the calendar for the weekend, which means there's no warning either, and Chicago has shown how it trades a headline from Moscow, Dublin or Washington.

**Marketing posture for the weekend: EXECUTE, on plan.** The question for Monday: the report was weak and the board held. What does your plan say about a market that won't fall on bad news?

• **Producers:** If you grow soft white, you own the strongest class in the report: commitments flat on the year while the others fell, and a Portland bid that has been above the board for three weeks. Check your resting offers against the Portland bid on Monday morning, not the Chicago headline. If you grow hard red winter, the commitments number is the one to know: 61% behind. Call the elevator Monday and ask whether basis has moved with the board, and what they're bidding for spring delivery. A $6.00 to $6.03¾ zone that capped three sessions is a place to have offers resting. EXECUTE says offers at your plan's numbers are live.  
  
• **Unpriced wheat in the bin:** The December hike was a what-if three weeks ago and is now the base case at about 85%. Run the carry with a quarter point added to the operating line. If the bin doesn't clear the interest at the higher rate, it costs you to hold it.  
  
• **Millers, feeders and end users:** A week that ended where it started, after a $5.82 to $6.03¾ range, is the kind where a scale-in works. Don't wait for the one perfect day. Hard red winter buyers have the better opening: it's the cheap wheat against Paris, and the world hasn't bought much of it yet.  
  
• **Merchandisers:** Sunday night into Monday, watch:  
  – Dec Chicago against $6.00 above and $5.82 below.  
  – The Monday COT report: it will show whether the funds covered into Wednesday's bounce.  
  – KC premium over Chicago, 35¼¢ at Friday's close.  
  – Paris and Minneapolis, when they settle Friday evening, and the KC–Paris spread against -$1.08.  
  – Weekend headlines from Moscow and Dublin.  
  
• **Lenders and elevator managers:** Mark unpriced Chicago-priced inventory at $5.93½, then run it at $5.80 and $5.70. Mark soft white at the Portland bid. Use a quarter point higher on operating lines for December as the working assumption and a half point as the stress case. With hard red winter commitments 61% behind last year, hard red winter collateral deserves its own line in the review.

Here's the part of this week that reaches the kitchen table. The same report that said the world wasn't buying much of our wheat came the same week as the inflation report that will help set the rate on next spring's operating note. The price of the crop and the cost of carrying it moved against the grower in the same days, for reasons nobody at your kitchen table caused. What you can do is what you did before any of it: know your number, know your bid, and know what the note costs at a quarter point higher. White wheat is holding its own while the other classes fall behind, and a plan that was written for a number, not a headline, still works. Somebody wrote the first marketing plan on your place long before there was a dollar index to watch. The tools have changed. The job hasn't: get this crop sold at a price that puts the next one in the ground.
