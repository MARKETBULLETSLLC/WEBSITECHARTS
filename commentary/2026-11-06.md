# CHICAGO BROKE. PARIS BOUNCED. RUSSIA'S FARMERS SAID NO.

Two weeks ago we wrote that the market heard half a sentence out of Moscow and sold it. This week it got the whole paragraph, with a city, a date, two delegations and an American in the chair, and it sold that too. Dec Chicago opened the week with a 3¢ bounce Monday to $6.06½. It gave back 6¾¢ Tuesday when Washington announced the Abu Dhabi talks, then drifted Wednesday and Thursday. Friday it lost 8¾¢ after a hot jobs report sent the dollar to a three-week high. Friday's low of $5.85 punched through Wednesday's $5.91 floor, and the close at $5.90½ didn't climb back over it. That puts Dec $2.04½ under the Sept. 2 high of $7.95, down 25.7%. By our count, it was the tenth straight losing week. Bloomberg called the eighth the longest losing streak since 1997. Nobody has named the tenth yet.

Every market we chart closed the week below its Box-o-Rox line: Chicago by 96¼¢, Kansas City by $1.12 ($6.31¾ against $7.43¾), and Paris by €11 (€225.50 against €236.50). And the three U.S. contracts are all riding down short-term trendlines drawn from their late-August high closes: 50 sessions in Chicago, 49 days in KC, 45 in Minneapolis. That is timeframe harmony, exactly what the Warm Signal is built to detect. This time the harmony is in a minor key. For a seller who has been in EXECUTE since September, that's not bad news. It's confirmation that the plan has been in phase with the market instead of fighting it.

**Now for the oddball.** Paris didn't get the memo. Euronext milling wheat traded down to €218.50 Friday morning, then turned around and closed at €225.50, up €2.75 and €7 off its low. Our chart converts that gain at Friday's exchange rate: about a penny a bushel lower in dollars, because the dollar's three-week high ate the euro gain. Fair enough. The bigger number is the one that should stop you. From the Sept. 2 highs, Chicago is down 25.7%. Paris matched its September high again on Oct. 14–15 at €249.75 and is down only 9.7%. The dollar index is up about 4% on the whole year. Currency can't explain a 16-point gap. Paris is the market that sits closest to the Black Sea and competes head to head with Russian wheat in North Africa, and it is the one least convinced that peace is coming. Chicago, carrying an 88,726-contract fund short, is the one most convinced. One of them is reading Abu Dhabi wrong. We start finding out which on Tuesday.

Here's what's actually on the table. On Tuesday, U.S. envoy Steve Witkoff said the U.S. will mediate talks between Russia and Ukraine in Abu Dhabi on Nov. 10–11, per Axios. The Kremlin confirmed that Vladimir Medinsky will lead its delegation. These are the first talks in about nine months. The announced agenda is post-war security arrangements, de-escalation and humanitarian efforts, per Fox News. Grain lanes aren't on it by name, and Putin still hasn't agreed to a ceasefire, per the Kyiv Post. Shipments through the Azov and Black Sea ports have been largely stopped since mid-August. Two weeks ago the market sold a "maybe." This week it sold a meeting. Neither one has loaded a vessel.

**We've seen this movie before.** In the summer of 2022, wheat came down hard on the road to the original Black Sea Grain Initiative and kept selling the idea of open lanes long after the deal was signed. Then, on the last weekend of October 2022, Russia walked out of the deal. On Monday, Oct. 31, Chicago jumped close to 6% in one session. Moscow walked back in two days later, and the jump went back where it came from. The lesson isn't that deals fail. The market prices the handshake early and the walkout late, and the walkout arrives all at once. Funds held 88,726 contracts short in Chicago as of Tuesday, per CFTC data as reported Friday. A stall in Abu Dhabi would meet a crowd that has to buy its way out. We aren't forecasting that. But a risk that no one is pricing is the one worth knowing about.

**Read the behavior, not the headline.** This week the most honest behavior came from Russia's own farmers. Moscow opened state intervention buying on Oct. 29 at 11,100 rubles a tonne for Class 3 wheat. That's about $138 a tonne, roughly $3.76 a bushel, per UkrAgroConsult. Only a fraction of the target volume traded. Too few elevators are accredited, especially in the south, and analysts had already said it would take at least 10 MMT of state buying to steady domestic prices against a program capped at 3 MMT. Set that farm-gate offer against Russia's Baltic export quotes, which sit far above it. Freight and handling eat part of that gap in any year. Much of the rest is what a blocked port costs a Russian farmer. And the Baltic route Moscow has leaned on since August heads into ice season this winter. So most of them kept their wheat. That cuts both ways, and both ways are worth saying out loud. While the lanes stay shut, that grain is stranded. If the lanes open, it comes fast. Stored Russian wheat is the real bearish case for peace, and it's sitting in bins, not on a screen.

Meanwhile the docks here at home went quiet. Wheat inspected for export in the week ended Oct. 29 came to 75,413 tonnes, about 2.8 million bushels. That's the smallest week since record-keeping began in 1983, and 78.5% below the same week a year ago, per USDA FGIS via UkrAgroConsult. Mexico was the top destination at 38,600 tonnes. Season-to-date inspections are 7.5 MMT, 22% below the five-year average, per USDA's Grain Transportation Report. Before anybody reads the obituary, remember that inspections count what ships and sales count what's sold. Net sales for the week ended Oct. 22 were 849,800 tonnes, a marketing-year high led by the Philippines and South Korea, and that wheat hasn't shipped yet. Net sales for the week ended Oct. 29 cooled to 540,000 tonnes. One record-low week can be a gap in the loading schedule. Two would be a trend. Monday's number starts to tell us which. And go back to Paris for a second: if U.S. wheat has fallen 16 points further than European wheat since Labor Day, buyers have noticed. The sales should follow the price. If they don't, that's a story too.

The scissors closed, but from the wrong blade. Two weeks ago, energy was pricing a wider war while wheat priced a peace. This week oil came down to meet wheat. Brent had its biggest weekly drop since 2020, more than 16%, on hopes for a U.S.–Iran deal, per CNBC. WTI settled at $90.83 Friday, about $11 under where it sat on Oct. 27. Wheat didn't rally to meet crude; crude fell to meet wheat. Cheaper oil should eventually cool the inflation that pushed the Fed to raise rates in September, but "eventually" isn't this week. The Fed held at 3.75% to 4% on Oct. 28 by a 10–2 vote, with Governors Cook and Hammack dissenting in favor of a hike. Chair Kevin Warsh said he'd wait for an "information-rich" period before moving again. Friday delivered information: 165,000 jobs against about 65,000 expected. The dollar index rose 0.72% to 102.28 and the 10-year yield climbed to 4.87%, per Rio Times. Every tick on that index makes a bushel of U.S. wheat a little harder to sell overseas. The Fed meets next on Dec. 8–9. Tuesday's midterms, per ABC News projections, also put new leadership over both chambers of Congress in January, which means new committee chairs on the farm and trade calendar in 2027.

**Our class held its bid again.** Northwest Grain Growers bid November soft white at $6.20 for Portland delivery Tuesday and again Friday. On Oct. 23 that bid sat even with Dec Chicago. On Oct. 26 it was 9½¢ over. Friday it was about 29½¢ over. The buyers behind soft white still want the wheat at this price, and the board keeps falling out from under them. Kansas City gave us a different lesson. On Oct. 27 we wrote that one strong KC session might be the first sign that the selling there was tiring. It wasn't. KC lost about 12½¢ on the week, nearly matching Chicago, and its premium over Chicago ended the week at 41¼¢, close to where it started. Funds added to their KC short. We called it one session at the time, and one session is all it turned out to be.

**Marketing posture: EXECUTE, on plan.** Don't let one Tuesday do the plan's job.

• **Producers:** The board is weak and the basis is strong. That's the exact combination a basis contract was built for: lock the Portland premium now and price the futures later. This isn't a call; it's a question to put to your plan. If you have unpriced wheat waiting on a better board, does a basis about 29½¢ over Dec argue for capturing that piece today? Be clear-eyed about the trade-off: a basis contract keeps you exposed if futures keep falling. Ask your elevator whether it's writing basis contracts at these levels, and at what fee. Resting offers stay where the plan put them, and the first overhead rung is now $5.91 to $6.02¼.  
  
• **Unpriced wheat in the bin:** Two weeks ago we said to run the carry against the note after the Fed. The Fed held, so the note didn't change. After Friday's jobs report, the chance that it changes in December went up. Run the carry again with a quarter point added to the operating line. If the ladder no longer clears the interest, the bin is costing you money to sit.  
  
• **Millers, feeders and end users:** The coverage decision belongs before Tuesday, not after it. Two events land on the same session. The WASDE is expected to be quiet; Pro Farmer puts the spread of analyst estimates for wheat at just 7.3%. Abu Dhabi is anything but quiet. Dec is $2.04½ under its Sept. 2 high with a crowded fund short underneath it. If your coverage is thin, extending it in scale before Tuesday is the protective step. If you're covered through the first quarter, there's nothing to do but watch.  
  
• **Merchandisers:** Watch five things this week:  
  – Monday's inspections: was 2.8 million bushels a gap or a trend?  
  – Tuesday's WASDE numbers for Russia and Argentina, not just the U.S. balance sheet.  
  – The first readouts from Abu Dhabi on Wednesday morning.  
  – The Paris–Chicago gap: if Europe keeps holding while Chicago slides, the export sales should show it.  
  – The dollar index against its Oct. 14 high of 102.735.  
  
• **Lenders and elevator managers:** Mark unpriced inventory at $5.90½, then run it at $5.85 and at $5.70. Run operating lines a quarter point higher for December. And plan for spring input requests to come in heavier than last year's. Here's why.

Retail anhydrous cost $1,007 a ton in the last week of October, up 3% in a month, and all eight fertilizers DTN tracks cost more than a year ago, per DTN. At Friday's Portland bid, that ton of anhydrous costs about 162 bushels of soft white, and that's before freight back to the farm. Oil came off $11 a barrel this week. The fertilizer bill didn't follow it down. That's the squeeze, measured in the only unit that matters at the kitchen table.

Now go back to those Russian farmers. Their own government offered them about $3.76 a bushel this week, and most of them said no. They aren't being stubborn. They have a number, and a reason to wait for it. The lesson for us isn't to hold. It's to have a number, and you already do. Yours were written before anybody set a table in Abu Dhabi, before Tuesday's numbers were printed, and before a jobs report moved the dollar. That's exactly when they should have been written. Some of those numbers say sell, and EXECUTE says those are live. Tuesday brings a negotiating table in Abu Dhabi and a table of numbers from Washington. Neither one knows what's on your operating note, or whose boots will be standing in that field after yours. Your plan does. Work it.
