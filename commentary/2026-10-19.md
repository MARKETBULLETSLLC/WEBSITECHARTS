On the last day of July, December Chicago closed the week at $6.56¼. Friday it settled at $6.60½. In between, the market ran all the way to $7.95 on Sept. 2 on the back of drones, burning grain terminals and a Black Sea that insurers stopped wanting to write paper on, and then walked every step of it back down. That's a $1.34½ round trip, and the ticket stub is on the weekly chart: seven weeks, seven lower closes. Think of a rally as a down payment the market makes on a story. If the story doesn't come through with a bigger bill (actual lost bushels, actual empty shelves), the market takes its down payment back. Last week it finished taking it back.

[chart: WHEAT_SRW_W | SRW weekly — seven straight lower closes off the Sept. 2 high at $7.95; Friday's close sits within 4¢ of the July 31 and Aug. 7 weekly closes]

**The runup to Friday was no runup at all.** Monday through Thursday, Chicago closed at $6.72¾, $6.68¼, $6.73½ and $6.69¼. Four closes inside a nickel and a quarter. The highs stalled at $6.81 twice and the lows held $6.62 every day. That's a market sitting on its hands, waiting for somebody to hand it a reason. A sideways box built inside a downtrend is a rest stop, not a turnaround, and when it breaks, it usually breaks the way the trend was already headed. Friday it did exactly that. No single headline did it. It was a pile of small ones: a tepid export sales number, a bigger Western Australia crop, Paris deferreds slipping under their own moving-average support, and a firmer euro, per [Total Farm Marketing](https://www.totalfarmmarketing.com/tfm-daily-market-summary-10-16-2026/). Dec traded down to $6.56¼, under the WASDE-day low of $6.61½, and settled near the bottom of its range. Markets that close at the low on a Friday are telling you who held the pen when the week got signed.

[chart: WHEAT_SRW_D | SRW daily — the four-day box ($6.62–$6.81) and Friday's break below it; next support is the Aug. 11 swing low at $6.45¼]

**Scout the terrain.** On the daily, the next real floor is the Aug. 11 swing low at $6.45¼, about 15¢ under Friday's settle. That's where the August rally lifted off, and markets have long memories for launch pads. Below that, the weekly 61.8% retracement of the whole October-2025-to-September run sits at $6.06¼. The other side of the ledger is just as real: [TFM](https://www.totalfarmmarketing.com/tfm-daily-market-summary-10-16-2026/) calls wheat "well into oversold territory." Oversold is a description, not an alarm clock. A market can stay oversold for weeks in a trend this clean, and this one has been clean.

**Ten days ago, the number that mattered was Russia.** USDA's [October WASDE](https://www.usda.gov/oce/commodity/wasde) had to close or defend a 6.3 MMT gap between its 43.0 MMT Russian export estimate and Sovecon's 36.7. It split the difference and then some, cutting to 40.0 MMT, a five-year low, citing Black Sea logistics. In any other October that's the bullish headline of the month. This October the same report raised Russia's ending stocks by those same 3 MMT, to 19.1, and world stocks barely moved: down 0.25 MMT to 276.04. That is the whole market in one line. Grain doesn't have to be destroyed to become unavailable, as [Allison Thompson put it in Agweek](https://www.agweek.com/business/markets/the-wheat-is-there-can-the-black-sea-move-it). But unavailable isn't the same as gone. A wheat buyer anywhere on the Mediterranean doesn't need Russian wheat to show up this month. They need to know it exists, sitting in a pile near Rostov, waiting for a hull. The market has decided the Black Sea is Russia's logistics problem, not the world's supply problem. That may be right. It may also be the most expensive assumption on the board if the next drone hits something bigger than a terminal.

At home the report was simpler and colder. U.S. ending stocks rose 23 million bushels to 740 million, about 19 million over the Reuters poll. Production rose a hair, and exports were cut 25 million to 750 million, mostly out of hard red winter. A short crop with a growing carryout is a customer problem. Last week's export sales didn't fix it: 11.8 million bushels (Taiwan, Sri Lanka and Mexico led the buying), with shipments at 11.9 million against the 15.4 million a week it takes to hit USDA's number. Commitments sit at 383 million bushels, 33% behind a year ago.

**Now the odd one, and it's the one worth your coffee.** Wheat and crude usually ride in the same truck. Fuel, freight and broad commodity appetite tend to lift both. Last week they went opposite ways in a hurry. WTI finished Friday near $100 a barrel, more than $10 above where it stood on Oct. 7. [Trading Economics](https://tradingeconomics.com/commodity/crude-oil/news/592494) had crude at its highest since September by Thursday on renewed shipping attacks around the Strait of Hormuz. Wheat, meanwhile, put in its lowest close in two months. The war premium didn't leave the building. It moved across the hall, out of the wheat you sell and into the diesel and nitrogen you buy. Analysts estimate about a third of the world's urea trade moves through Hormuz, per [the Western Producer](https://www.producer.com/markets/iran-conflict-drives-up-urea-prices/), and fall is when a lot of next year's fertilizer bill gets written. Picture a pair of scissors: one blade is the bid, the other is the input bill, and the margin is the paper in between.

[chart: NRG_CL_D | WTI crude daily — more than $10 higher since Oct. 7 while Chicago wheat made a two-month low]

**Count the rounds in the magazine.** Per the [CFTC](https://www.cftc.gov/dea/futures/ag_sf.htm), managed money was net short about 33,000 Chicago contracts as of Tuesday, Oct. 13. That's more than double the 13,144 they held on Sept. 22, and it's the fourth straight week they've added. Commercials have been buying the other side each week. Nobody's crowded yet. But a short that keeps growing into an oversold market is fuel lying on the floor. It needs a spark, and the spark doesn't have to be big. That's not a forecast. It's a reason to have your sell orders thought through before the bounce, not during it.

[chart: COT_SRW_W | SRW Commitment of Traders — managed money short building four straight weeks]

Kansas City is the mirror image, and it explains the spread. KC funds are still net long, roughly 17,000 contracts, but they cut 9,354 longs in a single week. That's liquidation, and liquidation is what a net-long market fears most when the demand story goes soft. Dec KC settled Friday at $7.04, Minneapolis at $6.76½. The KC-over-Chicago premium has shrunk to about 43½¢, from about 52¢ on Oct. 7. Two weeks ago we said hard red winter was a short crop being asked to take a pay cut. The pay cut went through. Merchandisers: watch whether HRW basis and protein premiums soak up the export cut before the futures spread does.

[chart: SPDINTER_SRW_HRW_M | KC–Chicago monthly spread — the HRW premium narrowing as KC funds liquidate]

Meanwhile, the only wheat market that gained ground last week was Paris, up €1.75 on the week per [Grain Central](https://www.graincentral.com/markets/daily-market-wire-19-october-2026/), with Dec at €243.75 Friday per [UkrAgroConsult](https://ukragroconsult.com/en/news/wheat-corn-and-soybeans-fall-in-chicago-on-friday-3/). Europe is the closest seller to the buyers Russia can't easily reach, and French planting is running late (15% done as of Oct. 12, against a 21% five-year average, per FranceAgriMer). Down south, Western Australia's crop estimate was raised 0.9 MMT to 10.9 MMT. That's still under last year's 13.3, but it's more wheat headed for the same Asian customers the Columbia River serves.

**Marketing posture: EXECUTE, on plan, not on panic.** For the 7:30 call:

- **Producers:** The next scheduled tranche goes. That's what EXECUTE means. But it means the *next* tranche, not the whole bin dumped into a Friday flush with the funds short and the market oversold. If your plan scales sales, scale them. A pop back toward the Tripwire, near $6.89, would be a gift to an EXECUTE plan, not a reason to tear it up. Have that order written down before the bounce shows up, because bounces off oversold lows don't send invitations.
- **White wheat in the bin:** Ask for the deferred bids, not just the spot. If the carry still pays, a forward contract for deferred delivery collects it without leaving the bushels naked to the next leg down. Price the grain, keep the bin.
- **Millers, feeders and end users:** This is your side of the market. You're looking at a two-month low and a growing fund short. Extending coverage while it's cheap is the long hedger's version of discipline, and a short-covering pop is your risk, not your friend.
- **Lenders:** Run the 2027 breakeven with $100 crude and today's nitrogen against USDA's $6.30 season-average price. That's the conversation worth having before the operating note is, not after.

Here's the part that doesn't fit in a bullet. Every generation in this business has had to farm through somebody else's war. Grandpa had grain embargoes, Dad had the '80s, and this generation gets drones over the Black Sea and tankers burning in Hormuz. None of us chose the market we inherited. What we do choose is whether we meet it with a plan or a hope, and the market spent seven weeks showing us what hope is worth: it sold for $7.95 on Sept. 2 and $6.60½ on Friday. The scissors are closing on both ends this fall: less for the bushel, more for the diesel and the nitrogen. Bushels priced on plan can't get caught in them. So before you pick up the phone this morning, put two numbers side by side: what your next tranche brings at today's bid, and what next year's crop is going to cost to raise. If the first can't cover the second, the question isn't whether to sell. It's how much of the risk you can afford to keep owning.
